Gear Design Series

Designed and modeled several common gear types in SolidWorks, including spur, helical, bevel, worm, internal, and planetary gears. The project focused on understanding gear geometry, transmission relationships, CAD modeling, and how different gear arrangements affect speed, torque, and motion.

01

Project Overview

This project is a self-directed mechanical design study focused on the geometry, operation, and CAD modeling of several common gear systems. The project includes spur, helical, bevel, worm, internal, and planetary gear designs created in SolidWorks.

The goal was to strengthen my understanding of how gears transmit motion and torque while improving my practical CAD modeling and mechanical assembly skills.

02

Design Objectives

  • Understand the geometry and operating principles of common gear types.
  • Model individual gears accurately in SolidWorks.
  • Create mating gear pairs and assemblies with appropriate geometry.
  • Study how gear ratio affects rotational speed, torque, and direction of motion.
  • Develop stronger mechanical CAD and assembly-design skills.
  • Build a foundation for applying gear systems in future robotic and mechatronic projects.

03

Gear Geometry Fundamentals

Before modeling the different gear systems, several fundamental gear parameters were considered. These parameters determine the size and shape of the gear teeth and ensure that mating gears can properly mesh and transmit motion.

For a standard spur gear, the pitch diameter is related to the number of teeth and module by:

d = mN

where d is the pitch diameter, m is the module, and z is the number of teeth.

The speed ratio between two mating external gears can be determined from their number of teeth:

ω1 ω2 = N2 N1

Other important parameters considered throughout the project include pressure angle, pitch circle, addendum, dedendum, center distance, and tooth orientation. The specific relationships vary depending on the type of gear being designed.

04

Spur Gear Design

The spur gear pair was designed as a 3:1 reduction using a 20° pressure angle and a module of 2 mm. The minimum allowable pinion tooth count calculated from the interference and undercutting condition was 15 teeth. A larger value of 18 teeth was selected for the pinion, giving the mating gear 54 teeth.

Np = 18,    Ng = 54

The resulting gear ratio is:

i = Ng Np = 54 18 = 3

Using a module of 2 mm, the pitch diameters of the pinion and gear are:

dp = mNp = 36 mm     dg = mNg = 108 mm

The tooth geometry was generated using the AGMA full-depth proportions and a constructed involute profile. The completed pair was assembled in SolidWorks to verify meshing and the intended 3:1 speed reduction.

05

Helical Gear Design

The helical gear pair was designed as a 3:1 reduction using a 20° normal pressure angle, a 20° helix angle, and a module of 2 mm. The minimum pinion tooth count calculated from the interference and undercutting condition was 14 teeth. An 18-tooth pinion was selected and paired with a 54-tooth gear.

Np = 18,    Ng = 54,    β = 20°

The resulting transmission ratio is:

i = 54 18 = 3

Unlike the spur gear, the teeth are inclined relative to the gear axis. The 20° helix angle allows tooth engagement to occur progressively across the face of the gear rather than along the full tooth width at once.

The tooth geometry was created using the same AGMA full-depth and constructed involute design approach used throughout the project. Opposite helix directions were used on the mating gears to allow proper meshing between parallel shafts.

06

Bevel Gear Design

The bevel gear pair was designed to transmit rotary motion between shafts intersecting at 90°. A 2:1 gear reduction was selected using a 16-tooth pinion and a 32-tooth gear. Both gears were designed with a 20° pressure angle, a module of 2 mm, and an 8 mm face width.

Np = 16,    Ng = 32,    i = 2

For a 90° shaft angle, the pitch cone angles are determined from the tooth-count relationship:

δp = tan-1 ( Np / Ng ) ≈ 26.6°
δg = 90° - δp ≈ 63.4°

The gear teeth were constructed around conical pitch surfaces so that the mating gears could transmit motion through the 90° change in shaft direction. The tooth profiles were generated using the involute construction approach used throughout the gear-design series.

07

Worm Gear Design

The worm gear system was designed as a compact 20:1 speed reducer. A single-start worm was paired with a 20-tooth worm wheel using a module of 2 mm.

Nw = 1,    Ng = 20
i = Ng Nw = 20 1 = 20

The worm was created from a 20 mm pitch-diameter cylinder using a helical profile. With a module of 2 mm and a single-start worm, the axial pitch was approximately:

p = πm = π(2) ≈ 6.28 mm

The corresponding lead angle was approximately 5.7°. A 20° trapezoidal profile was swept along the helix to construct the worm thread.

The mating worm wheel contained 20 teeth and had a pitch diameter of 40 mm. The completed arrangement provides a large speed reduction in a compact configuration while transmitting motion between non-parallel, non-intersecting shafts.

10

CAD & Assembly

Each gear system was modeled in SolidWorks using dimensions calculated from the selected gear ratio, module, pressure angle, tooth count, and other geometry specific to the gear type. The tooth profiles were created using constructed involute geometry and patterned around the corresponding gear body.

After the individual components were completed, mating gears were placed into assemblies to verify their relative position, center distance, shaft orientation, and meshing geometry. This made it possible to visualize how each transmission configuration would operate as a complete mechanical system rather than as isolated components.

The assemblies included parallel-shaft spur and helical gears, perpendicular-shaft bevel and worm gear arrangements, and a planetary gearbox consisting of a sun gear, three planet gears, a fixed internal ring gear, and an output carrier.

11

Design Considerations

Several geometric and mechanical considerations were used throughout the gear-design process. All gears were based on AGMA full-depth involute tooth proportions, with a 20° pressure angle used throughout the designs.

Tooth counts were selected not only to achieve the desired transmission ratios, but also to avoid interference and undercutting. The minimum allowable pinion tooth count was calculated where applicable before a final tooth count was selected.

Proper meshing also required consideration of pitch diameter, center distance, tooth orientation, and shaft arrangement. Additional parameters were required for specific gear types, including helix angle for helical gears, pitch-cone geometry for bevel gears, lead geometry for the worm drive, and compatible sun, planet, and ring gear tooth counts for the planetary gearbox.

These constraints demonstrated that gear design involves more than selecting a desired gear ratio. The geometry of each component must also be compatible with the mating gear and with the overall transmission arrangement.

12

What I Learned

This project improved my understanding of how gear geometry translates into practical mechanical transmission design. I gained experience calculating gear dimensions, selecting tooth counts, checking for interference and undercutting, and constructing involute tooth profiles from geometric relationships.

Modeling several different gear types also showed how transmission requirements influence the geometry of the mechanism. Spur and helical gears provided relatively straightforward parallel-shaft transmission, while bevel gears required pitch-cone geometry, worm gears introduced lead and helix relationships, and the planetary gearbox required several gears to satisfy geometric and kinematic constraints simultaneously.

The project also strengthened my SolidWorks skills in sketching, patterned features, lofted and swept geometry, assemblies, and designing mechanical components from calculated dimensions rather than relying only on predefined CAD models.

13

Future Improvements

Future development of the project would focus on moving beyond geometric modeling and evaluating the gear systems under realistic operating conditions.

Additional work could include performing motion studies to verify the expected speed ratios and rotational directions, evaluating interference and contact throughout a complete rotation, and introducing realistic backlash and manufacturing tolerances into the assemblies.

Gear-tooth stresses could also be evaluated using analytical gear-design methods and finite element analysis to investigate bending stress, contact stress, and the influence of transmitted torque. Material selection, shaft and bearing design, lubrication, and manufacturability could then be incorporated to develop complete gearbox designs.

A further extension would be to manufacture or 3D print selected gear systems and experimentally measure their speed reduction, torque transmission, backlash, and efficiency for comparison with the theoretical designs.
