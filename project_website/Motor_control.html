DC Motor Position & Velocity Control

This project explores the modeling and feedback control of a DC motor for both position and velocity regulation. Controllers were designed and tested to make the motor follow desired position and speed commands while evaluating system response, stability, and control performance. The project connects control-system theory with the behavior of a physical electromechanical system through simulation and experimental testing.

01

Project Overview

DC motors are widely used in robotic and mechatronic systems where accurate control of motion is required. This project focused on developing a feedback control system capable of controlling both the rotational velocity and angular position of a DC motor. The goal was to make the motor accurately track a desired speed or position rather than simply applying a voltage and allowing the motor to operate without feedback. The project was developed to apply control-system concepts to a physical electromechanical system and investigate the differences between simulated and real-world behavior. A mathematical model of the motor was used to study its response and design controllers in simulation before implementing and testing the control system on the physical motor. The main engineering problem was achieving stable and accurate motor motion despite the dynamics and limitations of the physical system. By measuring the motor's actual motion and comparing it with the desired reference, feedback control could be used to continuously correct the motor's behavior and reduce tracking error. This provided a practical way to study how modeling, controller design, simulation, and hardware implementation work together in a real control system.

02

Design Objectives

The main objectives of the project were:

  • Develop a simple mathematical model that represents the dynamic behavior of the DC motor.
  • Design feedback controllers to regulate the rotational velocity and angular position of the motor
  • Simulate the control systems and evaluate their stability, tracking accuracy, and transient response.
  • Implement the controllers on the physical motor and compare the experimental response with the simulated response.

03

System Design

The system uses encoder feedback to measure the motion of the DC motor and provide feedback to the controller. The same hardware platform was used for both velocity and position control, with the measured output compared against the desired reference to determine the required motor command.

Velocity Control Design

Velocity Feedback Architecture

The velocity control system uses a closed-loop feedback architecture to regulate the rotational speed of the DC motor. The desired angular velocity, ωref(t), is compared with the measured angular velocity, ωf(t), to determine the velocity error:

eω(t) = ωref(t) - ωf(t)

The velocity error is supplied to the PID controller, which calculates the motor control input using proportional, integral, and derivative actions:

u(t) = Kpeω(t) + Ki∫0t eω(τ)dτ + Kd deω(t) dt

The resulting control signal is applied to the motor, while the measured motor velocity is continuously fed back to the controller. This allows the controller to adjust the motor input based on the difference between the commanded and measured velocity.

Encoder-Based Velocity Measurement

The motor's angular velocity was determined from the encoder position measurements. The encoder count was first converted into angular position using the encoder resolution of 980 counts per revolution:

θ(t) = N(t) 2π 980

where N(t) represents the cumulative encoder count and θ(t) is the angular position of the motor shaft in radians.

The change in angular position between two consecutive measurements was then calculated as:

Δθ = θk - θk-1

The elapsed time between the two measurements was also recorded. The angular velocity of the motor was then estimated by dividing the change in angular position by the elapsed time:

ω = Δθ Δt

This produced the motor velocity in radians per second. The calculated angular velocity was then used as the feedback measurement in the closed-loop velocity controller.

Experimental Tuning

The PID gains were tuned experimentally by adjusting the controller parameters and observing the resulting motor velocity response. Different gain combinations were tested while monitoring the response of the motor to the desired velocity command.

The responses were evaluated qualitatively based on stability, oscillation, overshoot, response speed, and the ability of the motor velocity to converge toward the desired value. The controller gains were adjusted until the system produced stable and acceptable velocity tracking.

Formal transient-response measurements such as rise time, settling time, and maximum overshoot were not calculated during the original experiment. Quantitative performance analysis is therefore identified as an area for improvement in a future iteration of the project.

Experimental Velocity Response

The final controller gains selected for the velocity-control experiment were Kp = 70, Ki = 25, and Kd = 0. Although the controller was implemented using a PID structure, the derivative term was disabled in the final tuning, resulting in PI control.

The response shows a rapid increase in motor velocity followed by oscillations during the initial transient. As the response progresses, the motor velocity approaches approximately 8 rad/s and remains near this value with relatively small fluctuations. This tuning was selected because it provided stable velocity tracking with an acceptable experimental response.
